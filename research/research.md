# Complete Stock Research Checklist

Indian Equity Markets | Screening → Research → Valuation → Investment → Monitoring → Learning

I would organize stock research into seven stages, with separate decision gates. The idea is not to find stocks satisfying the maximum number of ratios. It is to understand why a company might outperform, what you are paying for that possibility, and exactly what could go wrong.

The checklist below is deliberately broader than conventional P/E–ROCE screeners. It covers long-term investing, discovering potential multibaggers, turnarounds, cyclical opportunities, and price momentum.

### Suggested starting thresholds

These are configurable research defaults, not universal rules.

| Metric              | Initial screen           | Interpretation                   |
| ------------------- | ------------------------ | -------------------------------- |
| Market cap          | ≥ ₹500 Cr                | Limit extreme micro-cap exposure |
| Daily traded value  | ≥ ₹1 Cr                  | Basic liquidity filter           |
| 3Y revenue CAGR     | ≥ 12%                    | Growing business                 |
| 3Y profit CAGR      | ≥ 15%                    | Earnings growth                  |
| ROCE                | ≥ 15%                    | Capital productivity             |
| Debt/equity         | ≤ 0.5                    | Conservative for non-financials  |
| Interest coverage   | ≥ 3×                     | Debt-servicing ability           |
| Operating cash flow | Positive in 3 of 5 years | Earnings quality                 |
| Promoter pledge     | Prefer zero              | Governance/risk consideration    |

Important: Do not use these same thresholds for banks, insurers, early-stage growth companies, or loss-to-profit turnarounds. Financial-sector companies require different metrics, and cyclical companies should be analyzed across an economic cycle.

SEBI's investor guidance likewise emphasizes understanding the business, financial statements, competitors, valuation, price-volume history and broader economic conditions rather than relying on a single indicator.&#x20;

## 2. Fundamental deep dive — once a stock is selected

This is where most of the research effort should go. I would divide it into eight categories.

1\. Business model and competitive advantage6 checks

What exactly does the company sell, to whom, and how does it earn money?

What percentage of revenue and profit comes from each segment and geography?

Is demand recurring, cyclical, discretionary or contract-based?

Who are the top customers, suppliers and competitors?

Does it have pricing power, switching costs, network effects or distribution advantages?

What could permanently destroy its competitive position?

2\. Income statement and growth quality6 checks

3\. Balance sheet and cash flows6 checks

4\. Capital allocation and management6 checks

5\. Growth drivers and competitive position6 checks

6\. Valuation and market expectations6 checks

7\. Market behavior and ownership6 checks

8\. External and event risks6 checks

## 3. Essential financial metrics and how to interpret them

A metric should tell you something meaningful about the company. The objective is not simply to maximize every ratio.

| Metric                | Why it matters                 | Warning sign                                             |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Revenue CAGR          | Business expansion             | Growth driven entirely by acquisitions                   |
| EPS CAGR              | Shareholder earnings           | PAT rises but dilution reduces EPS growth                |
| EBITDA margin         | Operating economics            | Margin expansion from unsustainable factors              |
| ROCE                  | Efficiency of capital employed | High only due to temporary peak profits                  |
| ROIC vs WACC          | Economic value creation        | Returns persistently below cost of capital               |
| ROE                   | Shareholder capital returns    | High ROE caused by excessive leverage                    |
| CFO/PAT               | Cash conversion                | Multiple years of profit without cash                    |
| Free cash flow        | Cash after capex               | Persistent outflows without credible returns             |
| Net debt/EBITDA       | Debt burden                    | High leverage and cyclically inflated EBITDA             |
| Interest coverage     | Debt servicing                 | Profits barely cover interest                            |
| Working capital cycle | Cash tied up in operations     | Receivables/inventory growing faster than sales          |
| Incremental ROIC      | Returns from new investment    | Expansion with declining capital productivity            |
| P/E, EV/EBITDA        | Valuation against earnings     | Paying growth-stock multiples for cyclical peak earnings |
| P/B, P/S, EV/sales    | Alternative valuations         | Used without profitability and sector context            |

### Special metrics by sector

| Sector                | Additional metrics                                                         |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Banks                 | GNPA, NNPA, NIM, CASA, credit cost, CET1, loan/deposit growth              |
| NBFCs                 | AUM, borrowing cost, spreads, collections, delinquencies, capital adequacy |
| Insurance             | VNB, VNB margin, APE, persistency, solvency                                |
| IT services           | Constant-currency growth, deal wins, utilization, attrition, EBIT margin   |
| Pharma                | USFDA observations, product pipeline, R&D, regulatory approvals            |
| Manufacturing         | Utilization, order book, capex, asset turnover, export mix                 |
| FMCG                  | Volume growth, pricing, distribution reach, advertising spend              |
| Real estate           | Pre-sales, collections, project pipeline, net debt, execution              |
| Commodities           | Cost position, capacity, normalized cycle margins, debt                    |
| Utilities             | Regulated returns, receivable days, tariff recovery, plant load factor     |
| Exchanges / platforms | Market share, transaction volumes, take rate, unit economics               |

This prevents false comparisons such as evaluating HDFC Bank using the same debt/equity limits as an industrial manufacturer.

## 4. Technical analysis — trend, timing and execution

For longer-term multibagger research, price action is useful for timing and detecting changes in market expectations. It is not proof of business quality.

| Indicator               | What to evaluate                             |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| 7 / 21 DMA              | Very short-term trend and pullbacks          |
| 50 DMA                  | Intermediate trend                           |
| 200 DMA                 | Longer-term trend                            |
| 50/200 DMA relationship | Trend alignment                              |
| RSI (14)                | Momentum strength and exhaustion             |
| MACD                    | Momentum shifts                              |
| Bollinger Bands (20, 2) | Volatility expansion/contraction             |
| ATR (14)                | Position sizing and volatility               |
| Relative strength       | Outperformance against Nifty 500/sector      |
| ADX                     | Trend strength rather than direction         |
| OBV / volume trend      | Participation and accumulation proxies       |
| 52-week range           | Breakout, consolidation and drawdown context |
| Volume profile          | Areas of historical trading activity         |
| Drawdown and recovery   | Risk of holding through adverse periods      |

A high RSI does not automatically mean a stock must fall; strong trends can remain overbought. Likewise, a price above the 200 DMA is not evidence of undervaluation.

For technical setups, I'd require a defined entry trigger, invalidation level, liquidity check and exposure limit. SEBI also distinguishes the long-term business focus of fundamental analysis from technical analysis of prices, volumes and trends.&#x20;

### Beyond the bull/base/bear cases

Test specific events:

- Business slowdown: What if sales growth halves for two years?
- Margin compression: What if operating margin falls from 20% to 12%?
- Valuation de-rating: What if P/E moves from 40× to 20× while earnings grow?
- Liquidity crisis: What if volumes disappear during a market correction?
- Debt stress: What if refinancing costs rise sharply?
- Dilution: What if the company issues 20% more shares?
- Industry reversal: What if government orders, export demand or commodity prices change?
- Governance event: What if auditors resign, pledges rise, or financials are restated?
- Lost decade: What if the business survives but its share price produces almost no real return for five years?

The purpose is to understand permanent-loss risk, not only temporary price volatility.

## 6. Investment decision checklist

Before investing, write a thesis that can later be proven right or wrong.

Pre-investment approval

0/9

I can explain the business and its main profit drivers.

I know why earnings could outperform current expectations.

I have verified financial and governance red flags.

I have compared valuation against peers and history.

I have calculated bear/base/bull scenarios.

I know which events would invalidate the investment thesis.

I have selected a maximum portfolio allocation.

I have a defined entry approach and review schedule.

I have recorded the evidence available today, not only the conclusion.

Record the thesis with the date, supporting sources, estimated fair-value range, risks, initial confidence and the conditions that would change your opinion.

A company can remain an excellent business while becoming a poor investment because its purchase price is too high.

## 7. Post-investment monitoring and learning

Separate three questions:

1. Did the business behave as expected?
2. Did the stock price behave as expected?
3. Was the original decision rational based on information available then?

This separation is essential. A profitable trade might have been a poor decision that got lucky, while a losing trade might have been justified by the original evidence.

| Frequency      | What to review                                                            |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Daily / weekly | Price alerts, abnormal volume, major disclosures, liquidity               |
| Quarterly      | Sales, earnings, margins, guidance, segment performance, thesis changes   |
| Half-yearly    | Competitive position, institutional ownership, management execution       |
| Annually       | Annual report, cash flow, ROIC, capital allocation, valuation assumptions |
| After exit     | Outcome attribution, thesis accuracy, decision errors, lessons            |

### Feedback loop without hindsight bias

For every decision, maintain a versioned research record:

Research journal — example

Initial hypothesis: Revenue growth will accelerate after new capacity becomes operational.

Evidence: Announced capacity, projected utilization, customer contracts.

Uncertainty: Commissioning timeline and demand realization.

Disconfirmation: Two quarters of delays or order cancellations.

Future review: Compare observed outcomes with the original forecast. Do not overwrite the historical forecast.

It is also worth recording whether a win came from earnings growth, valuation expansion, dividends, sector momentum or simply favorable timing.

## 8. Methods for discovering potential multibaggers

Rather than one giant weighted screener, I'd maintain several independent research strategies.

| Strategy                   | Primary discovery signal                   | Main trap                     |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------- |
| Quality compounders        | High incremental ROIC, reinvestment runway | Overvaluation                 |
| Earnings acceleration      | Improving sales, EPS and margins           | Temporary one-offs            |
| Turnarounds                | Operating improvement, debt reduction      | False recovery                |
| Growth at reasonable price | Growth supported by valuation              | Growth deceleration           |
| Cyclical recovery          | Cycle-normalized earnings recovery         | Buying peak earnings          |
| Price momentum             | Relative strength, trend, volume           | Sharp reversals               |
| Deep value                 | Discount to normalized value               | Value traps                   |
| Special situations         | Demerger, restructuring, spin-off          | Complexity and execution risk |
| Emerging small caps        | Market-share gains, capacity, scalability  | Liquidity and governance      |

For our multibagger study, I would start with quality compounders, earnings acceleration, turnarounds and cyclical recovery. These provide very different hypotheses, helping reduce dependence on any one market regime.

## 9. Testing whether a screening strategy actually works

Before relying on a screening formula, validate it against historical data.

The minimum research protocol should include:

- Point-in-time universes, including delisted and failed companies.
- Corporate-action-adjusted prices and actual trading liquidity.
- Financial publication dates to prevent look-ahead bias.
- Clearly defined holding periods and portfolio rebalancing.
- Brokerage, taxes, slippage and realistic execution costs.
- Out-of-sample and walk-forward testing.
- Comparisons with Nifty 500, relevant sector indices and simpler strategies.
- Maximum drawdown, hit rate, profit factor, Sharpe, Sortino, turnover and performance by market regime.
- Robustness testing across nearby thresholds, rather than optimizing a single perfect combination.
- A record of all attempted strategies to account for multiple testing and overfitting.

For multibagger discovery specifically, measure precision, recall, false positives and the median subsequent return. A model that identifies 10 historical winners but falsely selects another 500 companies is not necessarily useful.

## 10. How to organize the research system

Since you're also considering Excel for tracking the Indian stock universe, I would use a hybrid arrangement.

| Component            | Recommended structure                                   |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Stock master         | One row per company, unique identifier                  |
| Financial history    | Time-series financial statements                        |
| Price and technicals | Adjusted historical OHLCV and indicators                |
| Screens              | Separate saved screening strategies                     |
| Research dossier     | Detailed company-specific review                        |
| Valuation            | Bear/base/bull models                                   |
| Portfolio tracker    | Holdings, costs, exposure and risk                      |
| Decision journal     | Immutable dated thesis records                          |
| Backtesting          | Separate reproducible research results                  |
| Dashboard            | Candidate funnel, sector exposure, watchlist and alerts |

Excel is well suited for review, dashboards and decisions. For thousands of companies with daily historical prices, quarterly financials and multiple strategies, keep the underlying dataset in a database or analytics engine and publish summarized results to Excel.

### My recommended initial implementation

Build three linked checklists and maintain them separately:

A. Screening checklist: 20–30 automatable quantitative checks used to identify candidates.

B. Research checklist: 40–60 fundamental, valuation and qualitative questions completed only for selected companies.

C. Decision and monitoring checklist: 15–20 checks covering downside scenarios, portfolio construction, thesis updates and post-investment learning.

I would also assign each company one of four research states: Unscreened → Screened → Under Research → Approved Watchlist. Approval means research is sufficient to consider an investment, not that the stock must be purchased.

That structure gives us a repeatable framework for researching the previous 3–5 years' multibaggers and testing whether the signals can identify attractive opportunities today, without confusing historical winners with a reliable prediction model.
