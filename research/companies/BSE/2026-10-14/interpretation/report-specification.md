# Stock Research Report — Interpretation and Intelligence Layer

## Objective

Transform the existing evidence-backed stock research into a professional, investor-friendly research report that combines fundamental analysis, sector comparisons, historical performance, forward-looking scenarios, risk assessment and plain-English financial education.

Preserve existing evidence, formulas, source provenance, valuation assumptions, unresolved questions and counterarguments.

Do not sacrifice financial rigor for simpler explanations.

## 1. Explain every material financial metric

For each important metric, provide:

- **Definition:** What the metric means in plain English.
- **Formula:** How it is calculated, including accounting conventions.
- **Current value:** With reporting period, financial basis and source.
- **History:** Previous quarter/year and three-to-five-year trend.
- **Peers:** Direct-peer figures, comparable industry benchmark and rank or percentile where supported.
- **Interpretation:** What the number says about this specific business.
- **Limitations:** What could distort the number.
- **Assessment:** Improving, deteriorating, stable or insufficient evidence.

Use simple numerical examples when helpful.

Do not assert that a high or low financial ratio is universally good or bad.

## 2. Analyze financial statements

Cover the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement.

Explain not only what changed but why it changed.

Reconcile revenue, operating profit, other income, taxes, profit attributable to shareholders, EPS and operating cash flow.

Highlight unusual or nonrecurring items, working-capital changes, dilution, restricted cash, contingent liabilities and differences between accounting earnings and distributable cash.

## 3. Compare the stock with relevant peers

Select peers based on business model and competitive economics, not sector labels alone.

Compare revenue growth, profitability, capital efficiency, cash conversion, balance-sheet strength and valuation.

Use consistent reporting periods and definitions.

Produce tables showing company value, peer median, company percentile where meaningful, historical range, and interpretive conclusion.

Explain why a company deserves a premium or discount. Do not infer mispricing solely from a low P/E.

If direct-peer information is unavailable, mark the comparison incomplete.

## 4. Build a historical performance profile

Analyze a minimum of five years where reliable data exists, preferably ten.

Include annual and quarterly growth, margin changes, earnings quality, return ratios, capital allocation, shareholding trends, important events, share-price performance and valuation history.

Clearly distinguish secular trends, cyclical fluctuations and one-time events.

## 5. Build bottom-up future growth scenarios

Identify company-specific operating drivers.

Develop bear, base and bull scenarios over one, three and five years.

For every scenario, state assumptions for industry growth, market share, pricing, revenue, operating margins, capital needs, taxes, earnings and valuation multiples.

Calculate expected earnings and implied prices, with and without dividends where appropriate.

Clearly label scenario estimates rather than presenting them as facts or price targets.

Use management guidance and analyst consensus as separate inputs, not as unquestioned forecasts.

## 6. Analyze business quality and risks

Evaluate competitive advantages, customer concentration, regulation, governance, management execution, technology risks, financial liabilities and industry disruptions.

For each material risk, identify evidence, potential financial impact, warning indicators and thesis invalidation conditions.

Distinguish confirmed adverse events from hypothetical risks.

## 7. Make the report accessible

Every section should answer:

- What happened?
- What does it mean?
- Why does it matter for shareholders?
- How does the company compare?
- What should be monitored next?

Use contextual definitions, charts, comparative tables and concise interpretations.

Avoid unexplained acronyms and excessive analyst jargon.

Move calculation details and exhaustive source records to an appendix.

## 8. Research integrity

Prioritize exchange filings, annual reports, results presentations, transcripts and official regulatory publications.

Attach timestamps and citations to numerical claims.

Reconcile consolidated versus standalone, trailing versus forward, reported versus adjusted, and basic versus diluted figures.

Never invent missing data, peer averages, analyst estimates, growth rates or future prices.

Classify conclusions as VERIFIED, CALCULATED, MANAGEMENT CLAIM, THIRD-PARTY ESTIMATE, SCENARIO ASSUMPTION or UNRESOLVED.

## 9. Final outputs

Produce:

1. A five-minute executive briefing.
2. A comprehensive stock research report.
3. A structured financial-metric glossary with company-specific examples.
4. Peer comparison and historical benchmark tables.
5. Three forward-looking growth and valuation scenarios.
6. A prioritized risk and monitoring register.
7. An evidence and calculation appendix.

End with a conditional assessment of business quality, financial strength, valuation attractiveness, growth prospects and research confidence.

Do not issue a buy or sell recommendation when required evidence is incomplete.
